1. Decide what the website must achieve

Write one or two sentences: "When someone visits our website, we want them to…" Book a table? Request a quote? Find our hours? The main goal shapes every page.

2. Describe who it is for

List your main audiences and what each needs. A school site serves parents, students, and staff; a restaurant site serves first-time visitors and regulars checking the specials.

3. Sketch the pages

A simple list is enough: Home, Services, About, Contact, and so on. Mark the must-haves and the nice-to-haves. Fewer, clearer pages usually work better than many thin ones.

4. Gather your content

Content is the most common cause of delays. Start collecting:

  • Descriptions of your services or products
  • Your story, team details, and contact information
  • Frequently asked questions customers already ask you

5. Collect brand assets and photos

Your logo in the best quality you have, any brand colors or fonts, and photos you own or have permission to use. Good photos of your real work, place, or team are worth more than stock images.

6. Find examples you like

Pick two or three websites, from any industry, and note what you like about each: the layout, the tone, how easy it is to find things.

7. List the features you need

Contact forms, a digital menu, event listings, booking links, more than one language, an editor so you can update pages yourself. Features affect cost more than page count does.

8. Sort out the domain and hosting

Do you already own a domain, and do you have the login for it? If not, decide on a name. Keep domain and hosting accounts in your organization's name.

9. Set a budget range and timeline

A range is enough. It helps a developer suggest what fits, rather than quoting for something you cannot use. If a date matters, such as an opening or event, say so early.

10. Agree who decides

Name one person who gathers feedback and approves each stage. Projects slow down most when feedback comes from many people at once.
